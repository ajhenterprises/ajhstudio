Most website copy fails for the same reason: it tries to sound impressive instead of trying to be clear. A visitor lands on a homepage and, within a few seconds, needs to understand three things — what the business does, who it's for, and what to do next. Copy that doesn't answer those questions quickly is copy that isn't working, no matter how polished it sounds.

## Clarity beats cleverness

It's tempting to reach for big, sweeping language — "elevating," "transforming," "reimagining." But that kind of language rarely tells a visitor anything specific. Compare "We elevate your brand experience" to "We build websites for small businesses that need to look as good as their work." The second one is less flashy and far more useful.

Cleverness has its place — a memorable line here, a bit of personality there — but it should never come at the cost of a visitor understanding what you actually do. If someone has to read a sentence twice to figure out what you're offering, the sentence has already lost them.

## Write for the person who's scanning, not reading

Very few visitors read a webpage top to bottom. Most scan headlines, skim a few lines, and look for the next thing to click. That means:

- Headlines need to carry the meaning on their own
- Paragraphs should stay short
- The most important information belongs first, not buried at the bottom
- Bullet points do real work — they're not just formatting

This isn't about writing "down" to anyone. It's about respecting how people actually read online, which is different from how they read a letter or a magazine article. Good web copy is written to be understood at a glance and rewarding on a closer read — not one or the other.

## Every page needs a job

Good copy treats each page like it has one job to do. A services page should make the offering obvious and lead toward a next step. A homepage should orient a new visitor quickly. A blog post should be genuinely useful, not just a place to stuff keywords. When a page tries to do everything at once, it usually ends up doing nothing well.

Before writing a word, it helps to answer one question: what should someone do after reading this page? Call? Fill out a form? Keep browsing to the next page? Every sentence on that page should be pulling toward that one answer.

## Say what's true, skip what's not

The fastest way to lose trust is to make claims that don't hold up — invented statistics, vague guarantees, or results that can't be backed up. The strongest copy is specific and honest: what you do, who you do it for, and why someone should reach out. That's usually more persuasive than any amount of marketing language.

This matters just as much for a five-person nonprofit as it does for a national brand. A visitor can tell the difference between copy that's trying to sound big and copy that's just telling the truth clearly. The second one wins more often than people expect.
