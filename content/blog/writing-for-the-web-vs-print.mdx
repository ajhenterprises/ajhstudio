A well-written brochure and a well-written webpage often look nothing alike — and that's by design, not by accident. People read print and web content differently, which means the writing itself has to change to match.

## People scan before they read

In print, a reader has already committed — they picked up the piece, and they're moving through it in order. Online, a visitor is deciding in seconds whether to keep reading at all. That changes the job of the opening line: it's not setting a scene, it's earning the next sentence.

## Structure carries more weight

Web writing leans harder on structure — short paragraphs, clear headings, and lists that make information easy to scan. That's not a stylistic preference; it reflects how people actually move through a page. A wall of unbroken text that reads beautifully in a printed letter can feel exhausting on a screen.

## Links change how a sentence works

In print, a piece of writing is a closed loop — start to finish, no exits. Online, every link is a potential off-ramp. That means web copy has to do more to keep a reader oriented: clear context, clear next steps, and no vague "click here" links that leave a reader guessing where they'll end up.

## The goal is still the same

None of this means web writing has to be less thoughtful than print — if anything, it demands more discipline. The goal is identical either way: say something true and useful, as clearly as possible, to the person actually reading it. The medium just changes how you get there.

Good web copy respects the reader's time. That's the whole craft.
