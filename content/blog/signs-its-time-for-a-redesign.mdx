A redesign is a bigger commitment than a routine update, so it's worth being clear about when it's necessary. Here are five signs worth paying attention to.

## 1. It doesn't work well on a phone

If a site was built years ago, there's a good chance it wasn't designed mobile-first — and mobile traffic now makes up the majority of visits for most businesses. A site that's hard to navigate, read, or interact with on a phone is turning visitors away before they see what you offer. Tiny tap targets, text that requires pinching to read, and menus that don't work with a thumb are all symptoms of the same underlying problem: the site was designed for a desktop screen and never really adapted.

## 2. It's slow

Visitors don't wait around for a slow site to load. Neither do search engines, which increasingly factor speed into rankings. If pages take several seconds to load, that's not a minor annoyance — it's actively costing traffic and credibility. Speed problems often build up gradually: a few too many plugins, unoptimized images added over the years, hosting that hasn't kept pace with the site's needs. No single change causes it, which is part of why it's easy to miss until a redesign forces a fresh look at the whole thing.

## 3. Updating it requires help every time

If every small change — a new staff photo, an updated hour, a fixed typo — requires reaching out to someone and waiting, the site has become a bottleneck instead of a tool. A redesign is a chance to rebuild on a foundation that's easier to manage, so routine updates don't have to go through a request queue.

## 4. It doesn't reflect the business anymore

Businesses evolve. Services change, offerings expand, branding matures. A website that was accurate five years ago can end up quietly out of date — still live, still technically working, but no longer telling the truth about what the business actually does. Sometimes this happens all at once, after a rebrand. More often it happens a little at a time, until one day the site describes a business that doesn't quite exist anymore.

## 5. It's hard to tell what to do next

A visitor should always have a clear next step: call, fill out a form, browse services, schedule a visit. If a site is cluttered, confusing, or missing a clear call to action, it's not doing its job — even if it looks fine at a glance. This is one of the easiest problems to miss internally, because the people who work at the business already know what to do. A fresh set of eyes — or a visitor landing on the site for the first time — often sees the confusion immediately.

## What a redesign actually looks like

A redesign doesn't have to mean starting over with a blank page. It usually starts with a conversation about what's working, what isn't, and what the site needs to accomplish that it currently doesn't. From there, it's design and development, a review pass before anything goes live, and a plan for keeping the new site current — the same process as a first launch, just applied to something that already exists.

## When a redesign isn't the answer

Not every problem requires starting over. Sometimes updated copy, a few new photos, or small structural changes solve the real issue without a full rebuild. Part of a good redesign conversation is figuring out which one you actually need — and being honest when the answer is "not a full redesign yet."
